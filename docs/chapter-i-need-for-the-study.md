## Need for the Study

This study is undertaken in the Doctor of Philosophy in Business Management program with a specialization in Information Technology Management. That specialization advances the theory and practice of leading information-technology strategic planning and management in complex environments. It requires learners to examine collaborative relationships among IT and other organizational leaders, strategies that integrate technological arrangements with changing business needs, and the ethical and legal issues that shape IT management, so that practical solutions can be developed for real-world problems that arise as organizations compete (Capella University, 2024). The proposed project aligns with that specialization because the object of study is not a technical restore script. It is an IT-management governance problem: how U.S. small- and medium-enterprise (SME) IT and network managers handle competing recovery claims, allocate decision rights, escalate contested choices, and defend restoration when disaster recovery is delivered on an external platform (ITDRPaaS). Those are the same relationships, planning, policy, and life-cycle questions the specialization trains IT leaders to address.

Organizations must manage competing stakeholder claims and sustain legitimacy when they create and trade value (Donaldson & Preston, 1995; Freeman et al., 2004). Freeman, Phillips, and Sisodia (2020) supply the needed construct that updates that foundation for this opening: **value-creation stakeholder theory**. On that account, distinctive stakeholder work describes human actors cooperatively engaged in value creation and trade; treats values, norms, and ethics as mechanisms of organizational action; and reads many apparent tensions between stakeholder management and strategic-technical objectives as differences of narrative rather than mutually exclusive tasks (Freeman et al., 2020). In an ITDRPaaS incident those actors include executives, customers, providers, regulators, and the IT managers who must decide whose claim moves restore order. Stakeholder-salience theory then supplies the middle-range attention mechanism: power, legitimacy, and urgency shape whose claims count (Mitchell et al., 1997). Recoverability assurance names the IT-management outcome of that process: reviewable, decision-relevant evidence that prioritized services can be restored within tolerance (Lowry et al., 2025; Park et al., 2023).

What remains unknown—and what creates the need for this study—is operational rather than definitional. The literature already states that claims must be managed and that salience directs attention (Donaldson & Preston, 1995; Freeman et al., 2004, 2020; Mitchell et al., 1997). It does not yet show how SME IT managers translate mid-incident salience shifts into decision rights, escalation pathways, and evidentiary standards inside ITDRPaaS when disruption forces time-critical choices (Dorobantu et al., 2024; Mitchell et al., 1997). That unknown sits inside Information Technology Management: it is a problem of IT strategy, decision-rights governance, and collaborative leadership under technological dependence, not a problem of writing a better failover procedure.

The study is therefore needed for SME IT and network managers who must defend restoration to stakeholders; for the executives, customers, and providers whose claims compete during recovery; and for IT-management scholarship that still lacks an incident-level account of how value-creation stakeholder theory and salience are enacted when recovery is outsourced. The project answers that need through generic qualitative inquiry and critical-incident interviews. Freeman et al.’s (2020) value-creation construct remains on the Need / What We Know node. Mitchell et al.’s (1997) power, legitimacy, and urgency attributes remain on the Constructs node as sensitizing probes. Neither source is converted into a priori Chapter IV themes.

## Implied questions (unchanged; replace labels when official RQs lock)

- **PQ1.** How do U.S. SME IT and network managers describe stakeholder-claim management and salience shifts (power, legitimacy, urgency) during ITDRPaaS recovery or testing incidents?
